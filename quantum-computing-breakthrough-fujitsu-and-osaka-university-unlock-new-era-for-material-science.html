Fujitsu and Osaka University use quantum computing for materials

Published 25 March 2026 by Web Online Tools Editorial

Fujitsu and the University of Osaka reached a quantum computing milestone for simulating chemical materials, with uses in drug discovery.

In a monumental stride forward for computational science and quantum computing, Fujitsu Limited and the Center for Quantum Information and Quantum Biology at The University of Osaka today announced a groundbreaking technological development. This innovation is poised to accelerate the industrial application of quantum computers, particularly in the realm of complex chemical material energy calculations. This significant quantum computing breakthrough, revealed on March 25, 2026, promises to dramatically reduce the time and resources required for scientific discovery, impacting fields from medicine to environmental sustainability.

A Quantum Leap for Chemical Material Science

The joint announcement, originating from Kawasaki and Osaka, Japan, highlights the development of novel technologies designed to specifically tackle the formidable challenge of calculating chemical material energies. Previously, such calculations were either impossible with current conventional computers or would require millennia, even with earlier versions of the STAR architecture. The newly unveiled technology drastically cuts down computation time, transforming what was once an insurmountable barrier into a realistic endeavor for early fault-tolerant quantum computers (early-FTQC).

This development comes at a crucial time as industries worldwide seek more efficient ways to innovate at the molecular level. The ability to accurately and rapidly perform these intricate energy calculations is a cornerstone for designing advanced catalysts, optimizing molecular structures, and developing novel compounds. Fujitsu and The University of Osaka have effectively laid a new foundation for exploring the fundamental properties of matter with unprecedented speed.

The Technology Powering the Breakthrough

The core of this quantum computing breakthrough lies in the synergistic combination of two key advancements: the third version of Fujitsu's unique, highly efficient phase rotation gate quantum computing architecture, known as STAR, and a novel molecular model optimization technique. The STAR architecture has been incrementally refined, with previous versions enhancing computational scale, but this latest iteration, paired with the new optimization method, has truly moved the needle.

Researchers confirmed that this integrated approach significantly reduces computational resource requirements, shortening the computation time by three orders of magnitude compared to methods not employing this technology. Specifically, they demonstrated that computation times could be reduced to approximately 35 days with a qubit error rate of 0.10% and a remarkable 10 days with a 0.01% error rate. This level of efficiency makes quantum-enabled chemical calculations practical within an industrial context for the first time.

  • STAR Architecture ver. 3: A unique, highly efficient phase rotation gate quantum computing architecture.
  • Novel Molecular Model Optimization: A new technique that dramatically reduces computational resource requirements.
  • Accelerated Computation: Cuts down calculation time by three orders of magnitude.

Implications: Accelerating Drug Discovery and New Materials

The ramifications of this quantum computing breakthrough are profound, particularly for scientific research and industrial innovation. The ability to perform rapid and accurate chemical material energy calculations opens doors to accelerating drug discovery, a field often hampered by the immense computational power needed to screen and model potential new pharmaceuticals. Developing new drugs can take years, even decades, and this technology offers a path to significantly shorten that timeline.

Beyond pharmaceuticals, the advancements are expected to improve the efficiency of ammonia synthesis processes, a critical component of global agriculture and fertilizer production. Furthermore, it holds immense promise for advancing carbon recycling technologies, which are vital in the global effort to combat climate change and achieve sustainable energy solutions. These applications underscore the broad societal impact anticipated from this research, addressing some of humanity's most pressing challenges.

This means that industries can now explore a vast array of molecular configurations and reactions more effectively, leading to the creation of novel materials with tailored properties. From super-strong alloys to highly efficient catalysts, the potential for innovation is limitless. The core principle is that a deeper, faster understanding of molecular interactions directly translates to more intelligent design and more rapid development of physical goods and chemical processes.
