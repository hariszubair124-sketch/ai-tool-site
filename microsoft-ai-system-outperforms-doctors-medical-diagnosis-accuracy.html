Microsoft AI System Outperforms Doctors: Medical Diagnosis Accuracy

Microsoft's MAI-DxO AI diagnostic system outperformed general physicians in a study published in Science on 22 October 2026, achieving 83.2% accuracy.

24 October 20265 min readBy Web Online Tools Editorial

Microsoft's AI diagnostic system, MAI-DxO, has demonstrated superior performance compared to generalist physicians in diagnosing complex medical cases [1][2]. A peer-reviewed study detailing these findings was published in the journal Science on 22 October 2026 [1][3].

What did the study reveal about MAI-DxO's performance?

The study, titled "Sequential diagnosis with language models," evaluated the MAI-DxO system against generalist physicians using two sets of medical cases [1]. On 304 challenging clinicopathological conference cases from the New England Journal of Medicine, the system, when paired with GPT-5 and run without budget constraints, achieved 83.2% diagnostic accuracy [1][2]. In comparison, generalist physicians participating in the study, who had access to textbooks and the internet, scored 36.1% accuracy [1][2][3][4][5][6]. When diagnosing without external reference resources, physicians achieved 22.3% accuracy [1][3]. The single best physician in this group reached 51.8% accuracy [1]. This means MAI-DxO was "more than twice as accurate as physicians using textbooks and the internet" on the journal cases and "nearly four times as accurate as physicians" diagnosing without those resources [1][3].

The system also showed strong performance on 325 emergency department-to-admission encounters from the MIMIC-IV-ED dataset [1][2]. Here, MAI-DxO without budget constraints achieved 80.3% accuracy, while generalist physicians averaged 46.8% [1][2][3]. The best individual physician on these cases reached 62.5% [1]. Microsoft's announcement highlighted that the system "is 1.7 times more accurate than physicians" on the emergency department presentations [1][3].

Beyond accuracy, the study also considered the estimated cost of diagnostic work-ups [1][2]. On the New England Journal of Medicine cases, MAI-DxO, without budget constraints, had an average cost of $5,431 per case [1]. However, cost-constrained configurations of the system achieved 80.9% accuracy at $2,241 and 79.6% accuracy at $1,303 [1]. Physicians, in contrast, averaged $4,725 per case when using reference resources and $3,049 when diagnosing closed-book [1]. For emergency department cases, MAI-DxO achieved 80.3% accuracy at $4,979 per case and 73.2% under budget constraints at $2,634, while generalist physicians averaged $3,400 [1]. Microsoft stated that the system managed to reach the right diagnosis at "half or even one third of the cost" compared to physicians [1][3][4]. Across all evaluated approaches, no other human or AI system was both more accurate and less costly than MAI-DxO [1][3].

How does this diagnostic system operate?

The MAI-DxO system, also referred to as DxO, distinguishes itself from many existing medical AI systems by adopting a sequential, step-by-step diagnostic process [1][2][3][4]. Rather than assessing all symptoms and results simultaneously, it functions like "a panel of virtual physicians" [1][2][3][5][6]. This orchestrator system simulates clinical experts with complementary roles, who propose diagnoses, select useful questions or tests, and challenge emerging views [1][3][5]. It coordinates this exchange over several rounds of debate, continuously updating its assessment as new evidence arrives [3][4][5]. The system also weighs each test's likely value against its cost, applying cost-conscious test stewardship at each step [1][3].

The evaluation of MAI-DxO was conducted using a new benchmark called the Sequential Diagnosis Benchmark, or SDBench [1][3]. This benchmark was specifically designed to convert 304 New England Journal of Medicine clinicopathological conference cases and 325 emergency department-to-admission encounters from the MIMIC-IV-ED dataset into interactive, stepwise diagnostic encounters [1][3]. Each case begins with a brief clinical vignette, allowing the diagnostician to ask history or examination questions, order diagnostic tests, or commit to a final diagnosis [1]. Performance is scored based on diagnostic accuracy against a reference diagnosis and the cumulative estimated cost of ordered tests [1][3].

Two supporting agents are integral to each encounter within SDBench [1]. A Gatekeeper agent mediates information requests against the underlying case record and generates diagnostically neutral synthetic results if a requested finding is absent [1]. A Judge agent scores the committed diagnosis against a five-level physician-authored rubric, with scores of 4 or 5, representing diagnoses for which management would be unchanged, counted as correct [1]. Test costs were estimated using CPT-based pricing [1].

The system was initially developed with GPT-4.1 and subsequently evaluated with GPT-5 and GPT-5.5, with its accuracy advantage increasing over time without modifying prompts or orchestration [1][5][6]. Microsoft tested MAI-DxO with models from various companies, including OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, and DeepSeek, achieving the best results with OpenAI’s GPT-5 and GPT-5.5 [5][6]. For instance, with GPT-5.5, DxO boosted accuracy from 72% to nearly 87% on complex cases [5][6]. The orchestration method itself proved valuable, raising diagnostic accuracy by an average of 7.9 percentage points on the conference cases and 4.4 points on the emergency department cases across all models tested [1]. Interestingly, physicians asked a mean of 5.6 questions before ordering their first test, while DxO with GPT-5 asked a mean of 25.5, a strategy described by the authors as extensive structured history-taking [1]. Dominic King, a corporate vice president in Microsoft AI’s health unit, noted that "No patient ever came to see me with a nice summary," highlighting the importance of this step-by-step approach [2].

What are the limitations and future plans?

While the study's results are substantial, the authors and other sources acknowledge important limitations [1][2][5]. The comparisons do not demonstrate superiority over medical specialists, multidisciplinary teams, or real-world clinical practice [1][2]. Researchers anticipate that the performance gap would narrow when compared against expert specialists or teams matched to each case [1]. The study also notes that the measured advantages are amplified within a controlled simulation environment, which sets aside factors like uncertain patient histories, incomplete documentation, patient rapport, longitudinal follow-up, and multimodal data such as imaging and pathology slides [1]. Furthermore, the journal cases used may overrepresent diagnostically challenging presentations, and the comparison was against individual physicians rather than collaborative teams [1]. Neither the AI nor the doctors in the study had access to physical examinations or medical imaging [2]. The Judge rubric evaluates diagnostic correctness at the disease level, not therapeutic appropriateness, safety, or longitudinal management [1].

Despite these limitations, Microsoft is moving forward with real-world evaluations of the MAI-DxO system [3][5][6]. The company has announced a partnership with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston to test the system's clinical reasoning capabilities across different hospital settings [2][3][4][5][6]. In this prospective study, patients will receive standard care from human doctors, while those who consent will also interact with the AI system in parallel [3][5][6]. Researchers will then compare the AI's recommendations against the physicians' independent assessments [3][5][6]. Dominic King described this evaluation as "the biggest study of its kind" and highlighted the complexity of issues that arise in a Boston emergency department on a Friday evening [6]. He also expressed hope that the tool could help "remove 'the drudgery' from medicine" [4].

Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman views this work as a significant step toward building "medical superintelligence" [1][4][5][6]. He expects AI healthcare models to improve by "an order of magnitude" in capability within the next 24 to 36 months [5][6]. Suleyman predicts that by 2027, clinicians will have access to highly accurate AI predictions about how a patient's medical record might unfold, offering valuable foresight [5][6]. In addition to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center partnership, Microsoft is also collaborating with the Mayo Clinic to train a foundation model specifically for healthcare, with its release planned for next year [3][5][6]. Microsoft is open-sourcing the SDBench for select New England Journal of Medicine cases, including the benchmark, Gatekeeper, Judge, and cost-estimation code, to allow other researchers to reproduce and extend the findings [3]. King noted that "the biggest challenge in healthcare delivery at the moment is the growing mismatch between demand and supply of medical expertise" [6].
