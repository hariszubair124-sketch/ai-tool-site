US Midterm Elections: Voters Reject AI, Data Centers

US midterm elections on November 3, 2026, saw widespread voter backlash against AI and data centers, impacting key races and tech stocks.

5 November 20265 min readBy Web Online Tools Editorial

US voters delivered a significant rebuke to the artificial intelligence industry and data center developers in the 2026 midterm elections, with results on November 3 showing a widespread backlash [1][3]. This sentiment profoundly influenced key races across the country, leading to notable shifts in political power and market reactions [1][2].

What did the midterm elections reveal about voter sentiment?

Exit polls indicated that concerns over AI and data centers played a crucial role for many voters [1][3]. An NBC exit poll found that 56% of voters considered AI and data centers a “crucial or major factor” in their vote for Congress [1]. This sentiment was bipartisan, with 65% of Democrats and 39% of Republicans identifying these topics as significant for their Congressional ballot [3]. The election served as a "stunning capstone" in a year marked by mounting anti-AI populism [1].

Research by progressive firm Blue Rose Research highlighted the depth of public concern, revealing that Americans would rather live near a prison, a methadone clinic, or even a nuclear power plant than an AI data center [1]. While existential concerns about AI have grown, the firm's director of research, Ali Mortell, noted that the concrete local impacts of data centers—such as higher energy bills, noise, and environmental concerns—were far more salient to voters [1]. Mortell also observed that trust in institutions is low, making voters disinclined to believe reassurances offered by tech companies and politicians [1]. The shift in public opinion has been broad, with Mortell stating that “Opinion has soured with everyone,” not just within one political party [1].

How did this backlash impact election results?

The voter sentiment translated directly into electoral outcomes, particularly benefiting Democratic candidates [1][3]. Democrats invested over $100 million in broadcast advertisements, portraying their Republican opponents as overly supportive of Big Tech and data centers [1]. This strategy proved effective, leading to decisive victories in many races, including those in Ohio, Kansas, and Iowa [1].

Across the nation, voters approved more than 40 measures aimed at slowing or banning data center growth [3]. Counties from Edgewater, Florida, to Bloomington, Illinois, passed data center bans or moratoriums [1]. Mayors in Piqua, Ohio, and Yukon, Oklahoma, were recalled due to their previous support for data center projects [1].

Super PACs aligned with the data center industry and "accelerationist" AI development failed to significantly influence elections through their spending [1]. In contrast, "pro-safety" PACs managed to outspend them in key races [1]. Several candidates backed by these groups, including Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan and Manny Rutinel in Colorado, secured wins [1][3].

Notable individual victories underscored the trend: Annie Andrews in South Carolina defeated Darline Graham, who had been nicknamed "Data Center Darline" and advocated for a two-year moratorium [1][3]. Ohio Senator-elect Sherrod Brown won after criticizing his opponent as "the face of data centers in Ohio" [1]. Kansas gubernatorial candidate Cindy Holscher led her race campaigning for a pause on data centers [1]. Arizona Democrat Amish Shah led after pledging to "stop data centers" [1]. Wisconsin's David Crowley won after shifting towards tighter restrictions [1]. Democratic gubernatorial winners in Ohio, Texas, and Georgia also supported data center moratoriums or greater accountability [3].

The super PAC "Leading the Future," established with over $100 million to support pro-AI candidates, faced significant public skepticism [1]. A survey indicated that three-quarters of likely voters would be less inclined to vote for a candidate who accepted donations from an AI super PAC [1]. This backlash led big donors like OpenAI’s Greg Brockman and Ron Conway to withdraw previously promised donations [1].

What are the implications for the tech industry and policy?

The election results sent immediate ripples through financial markets [2]. Tech firms experienced a downturn on November 4, 2026, as investors reassessed the future of the AI boom [2]. Chip firms in Asia were particularly affected, with South Korea’s SK hynix dropping over seven percent and Samsung down 5.5 percent [2]. Tokyo-listed Kioxia dived more than 13 percent, Advantest tanked 14.7 percent, Tokyo Electron lost over nine percent, and SoftBank fell 6.8 percent [2]. Nasdaq futures in the US also declined by more than one percent [2]. Broader markets, however, mostly rallied, as investors shifted into other sectors [2].

Experts anticipate increased regulatory scrutiny [2][3]. Charu Chanana of Saxo Markets noted that a Democratic Congress introduces "another layer of uncertainty: regulatory scrutiny," citing risks including more oversight, data center restrictions, and capital spending uncertainty [2]. She added that congressional scrutiny could "accelerate demands for AI safety standards, independent model evaluations and greater accountability for data centre developers" [2]. Michael Kleinman, head of US policy for the Future of Life Institute, declared, "The AI reckoning is here," emphasizing voters' demand for "strong and effective guardrails now" [3]. Brendan Steinhauser, CEO of The Alliance for Secure AI, warned that the "AI industry is going to have to get ready to deal with it because I think there's a big deluge of bills at the state and federal level coming their way" [1].

While sweeping federal AI regulation might not materialize quickly due to the need for substantial Republican support to overcome a potential Presidential veto, politicians are likely to "think twice before siding with the AI industry" [1]. Congress could also move to undo previous Republican efforts to block state-level laws [1]. The Trump administration, for instance, had attempted to prohibit states from regulating AI, including signing an executive order in December 2025 that sought to punish states for passing "onerous" AI laws by withholding federal funding [3]. Despite the backlash, AI remains "strategically important for US competitiveness, particularly against China," with considerable bipartisan interest in maintaining American leadership [2].

What is the broader context of the AI backlash?

The voter backlash follows a period of growing public concern and controversy surrounding AI [3]. AI executives, including OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, have openly discussed "possible doomsday scenarios wrought by out-of-control AI" [3]. Communities living near data centers have reported concerns about public health, electricity prices, and environmental pollution [3]. A series of lawsuits have also brought to light "haunting and heartbreaking" allegations of AI chatbots influencing users into exploitative sexual experiences, dangerous medical decisions, "so-called psychosis," and even suicide [3]. Parent and education activists have actively lobbied to remove Big Tech, including AI, from classrooms [3]. Google's decision to effectively refuse to turn off AI search for schools using its classroom products further "rankled advocates" [3]. New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed what some advocates consider the strongest AI chatbot legislation in the U.S. approximately a week before the election [3]. President Donald Trump has previously encouraged the AI industry, stating that opponents of data centers "want to end up being backwards and poor" [1]. An October Politico poll indicated that nearly half of Americans identify Republicans as the party most eager to build data centers [1]. However, Democrats could also face backlash in the future, as opinion on data centers has soured across the political spectrum [1].
