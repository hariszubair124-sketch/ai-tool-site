China blocks Meta's $2 billion purchase of Manus

China blocked Meta's $2 billion acquisition of AI agent company Manus and forced the deal to be unwound.

22 May 20264 min readBy Web Online Tools Editorial

In a significant development echoing the escalating geopolitical tensions in the tech sector, China has officially blocked Meta Platforms' planned acquisition of Manus AI, a deal originally valued at over $2 billion. The regulatory intervention, announced on May 22, 2026, has sent ripples through the global artificial intelligence (AI) and M&A landscape, forcing an unwinding of the deal and leaving Manus AI to seek a substantial $1 billion in new funding to reclaim its foundational technology. This landmark decision underscores the increasing scrutiny on foreign investment in critical technology sectors, particularly AI, and signals a new era of nationalistic protectionism shaping the industry.

The Unwinding of a Billion-Dollar AI Acquisition

The blocked acquisition represents a stark example of how geopolitical forces are directly impacting high-stakes tech deals. Meta Platforms, the parent company of Facebook, had sought to integrate Manus AI's innovative technologies, likely to bolster its ambitions in advanced AI and potentially its metaverse initiatives. However, Chinese regulators intervened, ordering the unwinding of the acquisition.

This regulatory action has put Manus AI, a fast-growing company, in a precarious position, requiring it to explore a new $1 billion fundraising round to buy back the company. The narrative unfolding is a powerful cautionary tale for founders operating in a globally interconnected yet increasingly fragmented tech environment, where the intersection of innovation and national interest can derail even the most lucrative partnerships.

Who is Manus AI and What Was at Stake?

Manus AI, while not extensively detailed in public reports surrounding the block, was described as one of AI's fastest-growing companies. The core of its technology and its specific applications are not fully disclosed in the immediate aftermath of the block. However, the substantial valuation of over $2 billion indicates its strategic importance to a tech giant like Meta. The company's technology is reportedly partially embedded in other systems, further complicating the unwinding process and the reacquisition efforts.

The Geopolitical Chessboard: Why This Deal Was Blocked

The primary driver behind China's decision appears to be the increasing nationalistic stance on critical technologies, particularly AI. Governments worldwide are elevating localized AI capabilities to a matter of national strategy, recognizing AI as a new generation of national infrastructure. This has led to intensified scrutiny of cross-border tech acquisitions that could impact national security, data sovereignty, or indigenous technological development. The blocking of the Meta-Manus deal highlights China's commitment to controlling its technological destiny and preventing foreign entities from gaining control over strategic AI assets.

According to reports, two of Manus AI's founders were barred from leaving China while regulators investigated the sale, underscoring the serious nature of the review. This incident serves as a stark warning for US AI deals involving Chinese-origin technology, illustrating the complex regulatory and geopolitical risks that can ensnare founders caught in the crossfire.

The Broader Impact on AI M&A and Foreign Investment

This development is likely to have a chilling effect on future cross-border AI acquisitions, particularly those involving Western tech giants seeking to acquire companies with significant ties or operations in China. Investors will now need to weigh the potential for substantial returns against a dramatically increased risk profile, considering regulatory intervention a serious and unpredictable factor. The case is becoming a landmark warning for US AI deals involving Chinese-origin technology.

The global AI industry is already witnessing a shift where growth is moving beyond just AI model development to infrastructure expansion and real-world deployment across devices and industries. This makes control over foundational AI technologies and the data feeding them even more critical for nations. The incident also coincides with other significant AI-related regulatory movements, such as the ongoing legislative efforts in California to establish worker protections regarding AI and the discussions around an AI executive order in the US. Such events collectively paint a picture of a rapidly maturing and highly scrutinized AI ecosystem.

What's Next: A Fragmented Future for Global AI?

The future implications of China's move are profound. It suggests a potential acceleration towards a more fragmented global AI ecosystem, where national interests heavily dictate technological collaborations and market access. Manus AI's struggle to secure $1 billion to buy back its company after regulatory duress, with its technology partially embedded in another company's systems, highlights the difficult path ahead for startups caught in such geopolitical dynamics. It may also spur other nations to adopt similar protectionist measures, leading to a complex web of AI regulations and restrictions. Companies like Manus AI might even re-emerge as China-linked joint ventures before pursuing other avenues like a potential Hong Kong IPO. As AI continues to become deeply embedded across all sectors—from healthcare and finance to national defense—expect governments to play an even more assertive role in shaping its development and deployment, prioritizing national security and economic independence over unfettered global collaboration.
